4 Experiments
In this section, we provide the experimental results of the 
proposed model and performance comparisons with exist -
ing methods using several datasets. We ran all tests with the 
specifications described in Table 7.
We used three types of datasets: ‘ARIMA’ datasets, 
‘seasonal’ datasets, and ‘Yahoo! Benchmark’  [ 17] datas -
ets. The first two were synthetically created, and the theory 
and methods used in creating these datasets can be found in 
Section 3. The Yahoo! datasets are divided into four parts: 
‘A1Benchmark’, ‘A2Benchmark’, ‘A3Benchmark’ and 
‘A4Benchmark’. ‘A1Benchmark’ datasets are real-traffic 
datasets, and the remaining are synthetic datasets created by 
Yahoo!. In short, 96 ‘ARIMA’ datasets, 100 ‘seasonal’ datas-
ets, 67 ‘A1Benchmark’, 100 ‘A2Benchmark’, 100 ‘A3Ben-
chmark’, and 100 ‘A4Benchmark’ were used in testing and 
each respective score is displayed below. All datasets were 
labeled. Figure 3 shows examples of the final datasets with 
anomalies included. All codes related to synthetic dataset 
creation and anomaly generation are in the data.R file. To 
reproduce the test results, run run-tests.py in Python and 
run-tests.R in R and test results should appear in each 
test-results file respectively.
We now present test scores. We show our test results 
with various plots for reference. Since the anomaly detec -
tion task is a binary classification problem, our main metrics 
are recall, precision, F1-score and Type I error, as follows:

positive points, False Negative  is the number of wrongly 
classified negative points, False Positive is the number of 
wrongly classified positive points, and True Negative is the 
number of correctly classified negative points. Recall is syn-
onymous with statistical power in statistics. F1-score is the 
harmonic mean of recall and precision, providing a single 
value that considers the trade-off between these two values. 
Type I error, also known as the false positive rate, represents 
the proportion of normal points that are incorrectly identi -
fied as anomalies. Since these four metrics are often used in 
anomaly detection, we choose them as our main metrics. We 
also include the execution times of the tested models.

contributions. In the two cases (b) and (d) when it was not the

case (e), oGESD exhibited slightly higher F1-scores. In both 
ARIMA datasets and seasonal state-space model datasets, 
our implementation showed much better F1-scores than the 
other methods. In addition, due to its online learning capa -
bility, osESD shows faster learning in all cases compared to 
oGESD. sosESD exhibited the fastest learning times across 
all scenarios, which aligns with our expectations given its 
streamlined architecture. However, it displayed significant 
variation in F1-scores across different datasets.
Finally, osESD consistently proved better than 
norep_osESD, showing that our regression and replace -
ment steps do indeed have benefits.
Some results of the detected anomalies are presented in 
Figs. 5 and 6. One can easily notice that our proposed osESD 
implementation is the most accurate at finding anomalies.
4.3 Automated osESD
We also tested auto-osESD, a package for automatically 
learning the osESD parameters by grid search. We used 
the datasets described above, including two real-life unla -
beled stock volume datasets. Both of these datasets are from 
Yahoo! finance, and represent stock volumes of Apple [25] 
and Tesla [26]. Apple stock prices were set as from January 
1, 2001 to June 30, 2023 and Tesla from June 29, 2010 to 
June 30, 2023. This makes a total of 5926 datapoints for 
Apple and 3227 datapoints for Tesla. Figure 13 shows plots 
of both datasets.
Table 4 summarizes the performance results for the data-
sets used along with the parameters shown in Table 1. Test 
results can be reproduced by running run-auto-osESD.py 
in Python and run-auto-osESD.R in R.
Our codes used a total of eight synthetic datasets and 
two real-life stock volume datasets. The Yahoo! benchmark 
datasets were not used because they are too short for reli -
able tuning. In addition, since both real-life stock volume 
datasets were unlabeled, we did not include their scores in 
Table 4. However, as mentioned in 3.5, pseudo y values 
were generated in the tuning process and the best param -
eters were found.
The evaluation of the datasets revealed consistently 
high F1-scores across all scenarios. Notably, several data -
sets exhibited a recall score of 1, a particularly advanta -
geous outcome for real-world industrial anomaly detection
